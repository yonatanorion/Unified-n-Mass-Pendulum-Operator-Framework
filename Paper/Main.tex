\begin{abstract}
\noindent The linearized equations of the planar $n$-mass pendulum are organized by a cumulative mass-sum structure, $\sum_{i\ge\max(d,k)} m_i$, which we state in matrix form and prove directly from the kinetic energy. It induces the factorization $\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{E}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{L}$ of the mass matrix, from which the main results follow: the inverse mass matrix and the dynamic matrix $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{K}$ are tridiagonal in closed form, so the state-space operator is assembled in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations without solving any linear system; and the problem is similar to a symmetric Jacobi matrix, which proves that all natural frequencies are simple and that the $k$-th mode has exactly $k-1$ sign changes. The same structure yields a symplectic St\"ormer--Verlet integrator with $\mathcal{O}(n)$ cost per step and an $\mathcal{O}(n)$ energy evaluation. Numerically, the closed form is faster than the dense route by orders of magnitude and about four orders of magnitude more accurate at $n=3000$; the spectrum of a uniform chain converges to that of the continuous hanging chain at rate $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})$; and the integrator keeps a bounded energy error over $10^6$ steps and remains practical at $n=10^6$, where the dense transition matrix would require terabytes of storage. Beyond small oscillations, the rod tensions satisfy a tridiagonal system whose matrix is the angle-dependent continuation of the same structure; it gives the exact nonlinear accelerations in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations, about 4000 times faster than the dense formulation at $n=3000$, and a symplectic RATTLE integrator with $\mathcal{O}(n)$ cost per step, validated in the chaotic regime. Code and data are openly available.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} $n$-mass pendulum; small oscillations; tridiagonal inverse; Jacobi matrix; symplectic integration; nonlinear dynamics; RATTLE; hanging chain

\section{Introduction}

The pendulum remains one of the most fundamental systems in classical mechanics and continues to serve as a canonical model in multibody dynamics, robotics, vibration analysis, and control systems~\cite{lobas2005generalized,fritzkowski2008dynamics,fritzkowski2009dynamics,tomaszewski2005dynamics,kawaji1991control,plaut2013pendulum}. While the equations of motion for single and double pendulum systems are widely known, explicitly deriving the equations governing an arbitrary $n$-mass pendulum rapidly becomes cumbersome due to the combinatorial growth of inertial and trigonometric coupling terms.

\subsection{Related Work and Framework Positioning}

Several mature approaches exist for multibody equation generation and simulation. Featherstone's articulated-body algorithm~\cite{featherstone2008rigid} prioritizes recursive computational efficiency, achieving $\mathcal{O}(n)$ scaling suitable for real-time applications, though it operates implicitly through factorized spatial algebra. Kane's method~\cite{kane1985dynamics} provides a highly systematic approach for deriving minimal-order equations but typically requires manual formulation for specific topologies. General recursive formulations covering serial and tree-topology systems are treated in~\cite{jain2011robot,shabana2013dynamics}. Furthermore, general-purpose symbolic environments (e.g., SymPy mechanics) can derive explicit equations but often yield massive, unstructured expressions for large $n$.

Closed-form equations of motion for the planar pendulum with $n$ point masses have also been derived directly. Yesilyurt~\cite{yesilyurt2019equations} obtained the general equations by Lagrangian mechanics and induction; when regrouped, the coefficient multiplying each pair of links is the cumulative mass sum used below. Charles~\cite{charles2019lagrangian} gave a general Lagrangian formulation together with a small-angle analysis. The classical theory of small vibrations of chain-like systems, including the oscillatory character of their spectra, is treated by Gantmacher and Krein~\cite{gantmacher2002oscillation}. We do not claim the cumulative mass sum itself as new; our aim is to state it in compact matrix form with a direct proof and to exploit it systematically in the assembly and solution of the linear problem.

The present work pursues a complementary objective: \textbf{explicit structural transparency}. Instead of focusing on raw computational speed for massive degrees of freedom, we make the cumulative mass-sum structure the organizing principle of the matrix assembly and of the linear solution strategies built on it.

The resulting symmetric positive-definite second-order system admits the standard generalized-eigenvalue (modal) solution.  We use that solution as an independent reference and retain a state-space matrix-exponential implementation for applications in which a transition operator is convenient, such as control and repeated propagation.  The matrix exponential is evaluated by a scaled-and-squared truncated Peano--Baker (Taylor) series; this numerical device is standard~\cite{moler2003nineteen,higham2005scaling} and is not claimed as a new matrix-exponential algorithm. The conceptual pipeline is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\section{Lagrangian Formulation of the \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-Mass Pendulum}
\label{sec:lagrangian}

\subsection{Equations of Motion}

The dynamics of the system are derived from the Euler-Lagrange equations
\begin{equation}\label{eq:euler-lagrange}
  \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_j}\right)
  - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = 0,
\end{equation}
where the Lagrangian is defined as $L = T - U$, with $T$ the kinetic energy and $U$ the potential energy. For the $n$-mass pendulum,
\[
T = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i v_i^2, \qquad
U = g \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i y_i,
\]
and $v_i^2 = \dot{x}_i^2 + \dot{y}_i^2$.  The generalized coordinates are $q_j = \theta_j$, $j=1,\dots,n$.

\subsection{Coordinate Convention}

Each generalized coordinate $\theta_i$ is measured with respect to the downward vertical direction, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:n_mass}.  The Cartesian coordinates of the $i$-th mass are therefore
\begin{align}
  x_i &= \sum_{k=1}^{i} l_k \sin\theta_k, \label{eq:xi} \\
  y_i &= -\sum_{k=1}^{i} l_k \cos\theta_k. \label{eq:yi}
\end{align}
The origin is taken at the pivot, with the positive $x$-axis to the right and the positive $y$-axis upward.  This convention ensures that when all $\theta_k = 0$ (vertical downward equilibrium), $y_i = -\sum_{k=1}^i l_k$ and $x_i=0$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/n_mass_pendulum.png}
  \caption{General $n$-mass pendulum with the absolute joint angle convention. Each angle $\theta_i$ is measured from the downward vertical. The coordinate origin is at the pivot.}
  \label{fig:n_mass}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Explicit Derivation of Kinetic Energy}

Differentiating Eqs.~(\ref{eq:xi}) and (\ref{eq:yi}) with respect to time gives the velocity components:
\begin{align}
  \dot{x}_i &= \sum_{k=1}^{i} l_k \dot{\theta}_k \cos\theta_k, \\
  \dot{y}_i &= \sum_{k=1}^{i} l_k \dot{\theta}_k \sin\theta_k.
\end{align}
Hence,
\begin{equation}
  v_i^2 = \dot{x}_i^2 + \dot{y}_i^2 = 
  \sum_{k=1}^{i}\sum_{r=1}^{i} l_k l_r \dot{\theta}_k \dot{\theta}_r
  \bigl(\cos\theta_k\cos\theta_r + \sin\theta_k\sin\theta_r\bigr).
\end{equation}
Using the trigonometric identity $\cos\theta_k\cos\theta_r+\sin\theta_k\sin\theta_r = \cos(\theta_k-\theta_r)$, we obtain the compact expression
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v_sq}
  v_i^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{i}\sum_{r=1}^{i}
  l_k l_r \dot{\theta}_k \dot{\theta}_r
  \cos(\theta_k-\theta_r).
\end{equation}
Note that the double sum runs over all pairs of links up to the $i$-th mass.  The total kinetic energy is then
\begin{equation}
  T = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \sum_{k=1}^{i}\sum_{r=1}^{i}
  l_k l_r \dot{\theta}_k \dot{\theta}_r \cos(\theta_k-\theta_r).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Potential Energy and its Linearization}

Substituting $y_i$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:yi}) gives the potential energy
\begin{equation}
  U = g \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \left(-\sum_{k=1}^{i} l_k \cos\theta_k\right)
  = -g \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left(\sum_{i=k}^{n} m_i\right) l_k \cos\theta_k.
\end{equation}
For small oscillations we expand $\cos\theta_k \approx 1 - \theta_k^2/2$; the constant term does not affect the dynamics, so the relevant part is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:U_quadratic}
  U_{\text{quad}} = \frac{g}{2} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left(\sum_{i=k}^{n} m_i\right) l_k \theta_k^2.
\end{equation}
No cross terms $\theta_i\theta_j$ ($i\neq j$) appear because the expansion of each cosine depends only on its own angle.  This crucial simplification leads to a diagonal stiffness matrix, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:diagonal}.

\section{Structural Pattern of the Equations of Motion}
\label{sec:structural}

For small systems, the equations of motion can be derived manually.  However, the combinatorial growth of velocity products makes manual derivation impractical for large $n$.  The equations nevertheless share an invariant structural property (cf.~\cite{yesilyurt2019equations}): every term in the $d$-th equation that involves coordinate $\theta_k$ or its derivatives contains the factor
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prefix-sum}
  \boxed{M_{d,k}^{\mathrm{coeff}} = \sum_{i=\max(d,k)}^{n} m_i}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Cumulative Mass-Sum Rule as an Organizing Principle}

This property follows directly from the kinetic energy.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:T}
For the planar $n$-mass pendulum with absolute angles,
\[
T=\tfrac12\sum_{d=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} S_{\max(d,k)}\, l_d l_k\cos(\theta_d-\theta_k)\,\dot\theta_d\dot\theta_k,
\qquad S_j=\sum_{i=j}^{n} m_i .
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In Eq.~(\ref{eq:v_sq}) mass $m_i$ contributes to the pair $(d,k)$ if and only if $d\le i$ and $k\le i$, i.e.\ $i\ge\max(d,k)$. Exchanging the order of summation in $T=\tfrac12\sum_i m_i v_i^2$ collects these contributions into $S_{\max(d,k)}$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Applying the Euler--Lagrange equations~(\ref{eq:euler-lagrange}) to this quadratic form, together with the potential energy of Section~\ref{sec:lagrangian}, yields the coefficients listed in Section~\ref{sec:generation}; the velocity-squared terms arise from differentiating $\cos(\theta_d-\theta_k)$ and therefore carry the same factor $S_{\max(d,k)}$, and the gravitational term on joint $d$ carries $S_d=S_{\max(d,d)}$.

This quantity represents the effective inertia dynamically shared by joints $d$ and $k$.  It acts as an organizing principle because the identical cumulative structure governs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item diagonal and coupled acceleration terms,
  \item centrifugal and Coriolis-like velocity-squared terms,
  \item localized gravitational contributions.
\end{itemize}

Physical interpretation: when $k \ge d$, joint $d$ contributes to the acceleration of all masses extending from coordinate $k$ to the end of the chain.  Conversely, when $k < d$, the inertial coupling relies on the deeper segment of the chain.

\subsection{Explicit Example for \texorpdfstring{$n=4$}{n=4}}

Applying the rule to the four-mass pendulum yields the first equation ($d=1$), noting the proper dimensionality factor $l_1$:
\begin{align}
  &(m_1+m_2+m_3+m_4)l_1^2\ddot{\theta}_1
  + (m_2+m_3+m_4)l_1 l_2\ddot{\theta}_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) \nonumber \\
  &+ (m_3+m_4)l_1 l_3\ddot{\theta}_3\cos(\theta_1-\theta_3)
  + m_4 l_1 l_4\ddot{\theta}_4\cos(\theta_1-\theta_4) \nonumber \\
  &+ (m_2+m_3+m_4)l_1 l_2\dot{\theta}_2^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2)
  + (m_3+m_4)l_1 l_3\dot{\theta}_3^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_3) \nonumber \\
  &+ m_4 l_1 l_4\dot{\theta}_4^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_4)
  + (m_1+m_2+m_3+m_4)g l_1\sin\theta_1 = 0 \label{eq:j1}
\end{align}
and the second equation ($d=2$), scaled by $l_2$:
\begin{align}
  &(m_2+m_3+m_4)l_2^2\ddot{\theta}_2
  + (m_3+m_4)l_2 l_3\ddot{\theta}_3\cos(\theta_2-\theta_3)
  + m_4 l_2 l_4\ddot{\theta}_4\cos(\theta_2-\theta_4) \nonumber \\
  &+ (m_2+m_3+m_4)l_1 l_2\ddot{\theta}_1\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)
  + (m_3+m_4)l_2 l_3\dot{\theta}_3^2\sin(\theta_2-\theta_3) \nonumber \\
  &+ m_4 l_2 l_4\dot{\theta}_4^2\sin(\theta_2-\theta_4)
  - (m_2+m_3+m_4)l_1 l_2\dot{\theta}_1^2\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) \nonumber \\
  &+ (m_2+m_3+m_4)g l_2\sin\theta_2 = 0. \label{eq:j2}
\end{align}
The third and fourth equations can be obtained using the same methodology. 
Every coefficient is exactly a cumulative mass sum: $\sum_{i=\max(d,k)}^n m_i$ multiplied by the product of lengths $l_d l_k$.

\section{Algorithmic Generation Framework}
\label{sec:generation}

The complete symbolic structure can be mapped into a concise algorithm using the mass-sum rule, eliminating complex parsers or manual recursion.

\subsection{Matrix Assembly from the Cumulative Sums}

We define the mass matrix $\M(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ and the Coriolis/centrifugal vector $\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ through the relation
\[
\M(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + \mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) + \mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{0},
\]
where $\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the gravitational torque vector.  The cumulative mass-sum rule gives explicit formulas:
\begin{align}
M_{jk}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \left(\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^{n} m_i\right) l_j l_k \cos(\theta_j-\theta_k), \\
c_d(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) &=
\sum_{k=1}^{n} \left(\sum_{i=\max(d,k)}^{n} m_i\right) l_d l_k \,
\dot{\theta}_k^2 \sin(\theta_d-\theta_k), \\
g_d(\boldsymbol{\theta}) &= \left(\sum_{i=d}^{n} m_i\right) g l_d \sin\theta_d.
\end{align}
The sine difference alone determines the sign of every velocity-squared term; no additional index-dependent sign is present.  For example, when $d=2$ and $k=1$, $\sin(\theta_2-\theta_1)=-\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2)$, exactly as in Eq.~(\ref{eq:j2}).
For the linearized case (small angles) we set $\cos(\cdot)=1$, $\sin(\cdot)=0$ except in the gravitational term where $\sin\theta_d\approx\theta_d$.

\subsection{Algorithm for Linear System Assembly}

The following algorithm constructs the linearized system matrices directly from the cumulative sums without symbolic differentiation.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
  \caption{Linear Matrix Assembly via Cumulative Mass-Sum Rule}
  \label{alg:assembly}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require masses $m_1,\ldots,m_n$, lengths $l_1,\ldots,l_n$, $g$
    \Ensure mass matrix $\M$, stiffness matrix $\K$
    \State $S_n \gets m_n$
    \For{$j \gets n-1$ \textbf{down to} $1$}
      \State $S_j \gets m_j + S_{j+1}$
    \EndFor
    \For{$j \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
      \For{$k \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
        \State $M_{jk} \gets S_{\max(j,k)} l_j l_k$
      \EndFor
      \State $K_{jj} \gets S_j g l_j$
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Algorithmic Scaling}

The suffix masses $S_j$ require $\mathcal{O}(n)$ work and are computed once.  The subsequent loop assigns $n^2$ entries of $\M$ and $n$ nonzero entries of $\K$, so the assembly requires $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ work and storage.  Recomputing the sum $\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^n m_i$ inside every matrix entry would instead require $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ work and is deliberately avoided. The dense $\M$ is, however, not needed to form the state-space operator: Section~\ref{sec:tridiagonal} shows that $\M^{-1}\K$ is available in closed form in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations, whereas forming it by factorizing $\M$ costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$.


\section{Linearization and Matrix Assembly}
\label{sec:linearization}

\subsection{Small-Angle Approximation}

For small oscillations, we assume $|\theta_j| \ll 1$ rad.  The following approximations are applied:
\[
\sin\theta_j \approx \theta_j, \quad \cos(\theta_i-\theta_j) \approx 1,
\]
and velocity-squared terms (which are second order in small quantities) are neglected.  Under these assumptions the equations of motion reduce to the linear system
\begin{equation}
\M \ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + \K \boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{0},
\end{equation}
where $\M$ and $\K$ are constant matrices.

\subsection{Derivation of the Mass Matrix}

Inserting the small-angle approximation $\cos(\theta_j-\theta_k)\approx 1$ into the general expression for $M_{jk}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ yields the constant mass matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mjk}
  M_{jk} = \left(\sum_{i=\max(j,k)}^{n} m_i\right) l_j l_k.
\end{equation}
Note that $M_{jk}=M_{kj}$ because $\max(j,k)=\max(k,j)$.  Positive definiteness follows from a factorization: writing $\mathbf{L}=\operatorname{diag}(l_1,\ldots,l_n)$, $\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(m_1,\ldots,m_n)$ and $\mathbf{E}$ for the lower-triangular matrix of ones ($E_{ij}=1$ for $i\ge j$), Proposition~\ref{prop:T} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M-factor}
  \M=\mathbf{L}\,\mathbf{E}\trans\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{L},
\end{equation}
since $(\mathbf{E}\trans\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E})_{jk}=\sum_i m_i E_{ij}E_{ik}=S_{\max(j,k)}$. Because $\mathbf{D}$ is positive definite and $\mathbf{E}\mathbf{L}$ is invertible for positive masses and lengths, $\M$ is symmetric positive definite.

\subsection{Diagonal Structure of the Gravitational Stiffness Matrix}
\label{sec:diagonal}

From the potential energy expansion (Eq.~(\ref{eq:U_quadratic})), the gravitational torque on joint $d$ is
\[
g_d = \frac{\partial U_{\text{quad}}}{\partial \theta_d}
= g \left(\sum_{i=d}^{n} m_i\right) l_d \theta_d.
\]
Therefore the stiffness matrix $\K$ is diagonal:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kjk}
  K_{jk} = \left(\sum_{i=j}^{n} m_i\right) g l_j \,\delta_{jk}.
\end{equation}
No off-diagonal entries appear because the quadratic potential contains only $\theta_j^2$ terms; the coordinate convention (angles measured from the downward vertical) eliminates $\theta_i\theta_j$ cross couplings.

Because $\M$ and $\K$ are symmetric positive definite for positive masses and lengths, the generalized eigenproblem admits an $\M$-orthonormal modal matrix $\mathbf{U}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modal-eigenproblem}
  \K\mathbf{U}=\M\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2},\qquad
  \mathbf{U}\trans\M\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I},\qquad
  \mathbf{U}\trans\K\mathbf{U}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{2},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n)$. Since both $\M$ and $\K$ are positive definite, all natural frequencies are real and strictly positive, so there are neither zero-frequency rigid-body modes nor unstable roots. Moreover, the frequencies are simple, $0<\omega_1<\omega_2<\dots<\omega_n$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum} below). With $\boldsymbol{\theta}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{q}$, the system decouples into $\ddot q_j+\omega_j^2q_j=0$, with the exact solution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:modal-solution}
  q_j(t)=q_j(0)\cos(\omega_j t)
  +\frac{\dot q_j(0)}{\omega_j}\sin(\omega_j t).
\end{equation}
This is the natural direct solution for the unforced linear second-order problem and is used below as an independent numerical reference.

\subsection{Closed-Form Inverse Mass Matrix and Tridiagonal Dynamic Matrix}
\label{sec:tridiagonal}

Although $\M$ is dense, its inverse is not. Throughout, $1/m_0:=0$ and entries not listed are zero.

\begin{theorem}[Closed-form inverse mass matrix]\label{thm:inverse}
For positive masses and lengths, $\M^{-1}$ is the symmetric tridiagonal matrix with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Minv}
  (\M^{-1})_{jj}=\frac{1}{l_j^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{m_j}+\frac{1}{m_{j-1}}\right),\qquad
  (\M^{-1})_{j,j-1}=(\M^{-1})_{j-1,j}=-\frac{1}{m_{j-1}\,l_{j-1}\,l_j}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Eq.~(\ref{eq:M-factor}), $\M^{-1}=\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{-\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{L}^{-1}$. Since $\mathbf{E}$ forms cumulative sums, $\mathbf{E}^{-1}$ forms backward differences: it is lower bidiagonal with $(\mathbf{E}^{-1})_{jj}=1$ and $(\mathbf{E}^{-1})_{j,j-1}=-1$. Hence
$(\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{-\mathsf{T}})_{jk}=\sum_i(\mathbf{E}^{-1})_{ji}(\mathbf{E}^{-1})_{ki}/m_i$
vanishes for $|j-k|>1$, equals $1/m_j+1/m_{j-1}$ for $k=j$ and $-1/m_{j-1}$ for $k=j-1$. Scaling by $\mathbf{L}^{-1}$ on both sides gives Eq.~(\ref{eq:Minv}).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The matrix $(S_{\max(j,k)})_{jk}=\mathbf{E}\trans\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}$ is a single-pair (Green's) matrix, and the tridiagonality of inverses of such matrices is classical~\cite{gantmacher2002oscillation,meurant1992review}. To our knowledge, its explicit consequences for the $n$-mass pendulum stated below have not been exploited in the pendulum literature, where the dense $\M$ is assembled and factorized~\cite{yesilyurt2019equations,charles2019lagrangian,bondada2023dynamics}.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Tridiagonal dynamic matrix]\label{cor:Q}
The dynamic matrix $\mathbf{Q}=\M^{-1}\K$ is tridiagonal with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
  Q_{jj}=\frac{g\,S_j}{l_j}\left(\frac{1}{m_j}+\frac{1}{m_{j-1}}\right),\qquad
  Q_{j,j-1}=-\frac{g\,S_{j-1}}{m_{j-1}\,l_j},\qquad
  Q_{j-1,j}=-\frac{g\,S_{j}}{m_{j-1}\,l_{j-1}}.
\end{equation}
Consequently the state-space operator $\A$ is assembled in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations and storage, and $\A\Rstate$ costs $\mathcal{O}(n)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Multiply Eq.~(\ref{eq:Minv}) on the right by the diagonal matrix $\K=g\operatorname{diag}(S_jl_j)$.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:Q} has a transparent physical meaning: in the linearized equations solved for the accelerations, link $j$ couples only to its neighbours $j\pm1$, as expected from a Newtonian picture in which each mass interacts only through the two adjacent rods, whose equilibrium tensions are $gS_j$ and $gS_{j+1}$. The dense coupling of $\M$ is an artifact of the absolute-angle Lagrangian description; it disappears upon inversion.

\begin{theorem}[Spectral structure]\label{thm:spectrum}
Let $\K^{1/2}=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sqrt{gS_jl_j}\bigr)$ and $\mathbf{J}=\K^{1/2}\M^{-1}\K^{1/2}$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] $\mathbf{J}$ is symmetric tridiagonal with $J_{jj}=Q_{jj}$ and
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:Jacobi}
    J_{j,j-1}=J_{j-1,j}=-\frac{g\sqrt{S_{j-1}S_j}}{m_{j-1}\sqrt{l_{j-1}l_j}}<0;
  \end{equation}
  \item[(ii)] $\K\mathbf{u}=\omega^2\M\mathbf{u}$ if and only if $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{w}=\omega^2\mathbf{w}$ with $\mathbf{u}=\K^{-1/2}\mathbf{w}$;
  \item[(iii)] all natural frequencies are simple: $0<\omega_1<\omega_2<\dots<\omega_n$;
  \item[(iv)] the $k$-th mode shape $\mathbf{u}^{(k)}$ has exactly $k-1$ sign changes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(i) follows from Eq.~(\ref{eq:Minv}) and the diagonal form of $\K^{1/2}$. (ii) $\M^{-1}\K\mathbf{u}=\omega^2\mathbf{u}$ is equivalent to $\K^{1/2}\M^{-1}\K^{1/2}(\K^{1/2}\mathbf{u})=\omega^2\K^{1/2}\mathbf{u}$. (iii) $\mathbf{J}$ is congruent to $\M^{-1}$, hence positive definite. Because every off-diagonal entry is non-zero, the rows of $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{w}=\lambda\mathbf{w}$ form a three-term recurrence that determines $\mathbf{w}$ uniquely from $w_1$; every eigenspace is therefore one-dimensional, and since $\mathbf{J}$ is symmetric all eigenvalues are simple. (iv) For a Jacobi matrix with negative off-diagonal entries, the eigenvector of the $k$-th smallest eigenvalue has exactly $k-1$ sign changes~\cite[Ch.~II]{gantmacher2002oscillation}; multiplication by the positive diagonal matrix $\K^{-1/2}$ preserves signs.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Numerical aspects]
High-frequency modes are strongly localized: for random chains with $n\approx 20$--$40$ some components fall below $10^{-20}$ relative to the largest, so the sign pattern in (iv) cannot be resolved in double precision. We verified (iv) in 80-digit arithmetic for 849 modes of 60 random chains ($2\le n\le24$) with no exception. Eigenvalues computed from $\mathbf{J}$ with a tridiagonal solver are not affected by this localization.
\end{remark}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
  \caption{$\mathcal{O}(n)$ assembly of the dynamic and Jacobi matrices}
  \label{alg:tridiagonal}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require masses $m_1,\ldots,m_n$, lengths $l_1,\ldots,l_n$, $g$
    \Ensure tridiagonal $\mathbf{Q}=\M^{-1}\K$ and Jacobi matrix $\mathbf{J}$
    \State $S_n \gets m_n$; \textbf{for} $j\gets n-1$ \textbf{down to} $1$: $S_j\gets m_j+S_{j+1}$
    \For{$j \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
      \State $Q_{jj}\gets J_{jj}\gets g S_j\,(1/m_j+1/m_{j-1})/l_j$ \Comment{$1/m_0:=0$}
      \If{$j>1$}
        \State $Q_{j,j-1}\gets -gS_{j-1}/(m_{j-1}l_j)$;\quad $Q_{j-1,j}\gets -gS_j/(m_{j-1}l_{j-1})$
        \State $J_{j,j-1}\gets J_{j-1,j}\gets -g\sqrt{S_{j-1}S_j}/(m_{j-1}\sqrt{l_{j-1}l_j})$
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

All natural frequencies then follow from a symmetric tridiagonal eigensolver in $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ operations, and any selected subset (e.g.\ the lowest $p$ modes, by bisection) in $\mathcal{O}(np)$, compared with $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ for the dense generalized eigenproblem.

\subsection{State-Space Representation and Control Readiness}

Define the state vector $\Rstate(t) = (\boldsymbol{\theta}(t), \dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t))\trans \in \R^{2n}$.  The linearized system can then be written as
\begin{equation}
\dot{\Rstate} = \A \Rstate, \qquad
\A = \begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf{0} & \mathbf{I} \\
-\M^{-1}\K & \mathbf{0}
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
whose lower-left block is the tridiagonal matrix $-\mathbf{Q}$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:Q}.
The matrix $\A$ is constant and possesses purely imaginary conjugate eigenvalue pairs $\pm i\omega_j$, reflecting the undamped Hamiltonian structure of small oscillations. This first-order form is not required for the modal solution; it is retained because a reusable transition operator is directly compatible with modern state-space control frameworks, observer/Kalman filter design, and linear-quadratic regulation (LQR) where input matrices $\mathbf{B}_u$ are coupled to the equations of motion.

\section{Operator-Based Dynamic Propagation}
\label{sec:operator}

\subsection{Peano-Baker Series for the Transition Matrix}

Because $\A$ is constant, the state transition matrix from $0$ to $t$
is the matrix exponential $\Phi(t,0)=e^{\A t}$.

For the autonomous system considered here, the Peano--Baker series is
exactly the Taylor series of the matrix exponential; the distinction
becomes relevant only for time-dependent operators, where the
Peano--Baker and Magnus expansions differ~\cite{magnus1954exponential,blanes2009magnus}.  A direct truncated
Taylor series can be inaccurate when $\|\A\Delta t\|$ is large
\cite{moler2003nineteen}, so the implementation uses scaling and squaring.

A convenient implementation defines $\mathbf{X}=\A\Delta t$ and chooses
$s=\max\{0,\lceil\log_2(\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\infty}/\eta)\rceil\}$ with
$\eta=1/2$.  For $\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{X}/2^s$, the approximation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:peano-truncated}
  \Phi(\Delta t) \approx
  \left(\sum_{k=0}^{N} \frac{\mathbf{B}^k}{k!}\right)^{2^s}.
\end{equation}
Scaling controls the Taylor remainder before repeated squaring.  With $N=20$, the tested systems agree with both \texttt{scipy.linalg.expm} and the modal solution to double-precision round-off.  This implementation is included for transparency; robust library routines based on established matrix-exponential algorithms, such as scaling and squaring with Pad\'e approximants~\cite{higham2005scaling}, should be preferred outside the tested parameter range.

\subsection{Propagation Algorithm and Reusability}

Once $\Phi(\Delta t)$ is precomputed (using the scaled series), the linear propagation over a time step is simply
\[
\Rstate(t+\Delta t) = \Phi(\Delta t) \Rstate(t).
\]
This requires only matrix-vector multiplications, making it highly efficient for repeated time stepping. Importantly, because $\Phi(\Delta t)$ is constant for fixed system parameters and time step, it can be precomputed once and reused across many simulations (e.g., parameter sweeps, Monte Carlo runs, or real-time control loops). This reusability is a key practical advantage of the operator-based formulation.

\subsection{Structure-Preserving \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{O}(n)$}{O(n)} Time Stepping}
\label{sec:verlet}

The transition matrix $\Phi(\Delta t)$ is dense: each step costs $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$, its computation $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$, and its storage $8(2n)^2$ bytes. For large $n$ the tridiagonal form of Corollary~\ref{cor:Q} offers a cheaper route. Writing the linear equations as $\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta}=-\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\theta$, the St\"ormer--Verlet (leapfrog) scheme reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:verlet}
  \dot{\boldsymbol\theta}^{k+1/2}=\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}^{k}-\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\theta^{k},\qquad
  \boldsymbol\theta^{k+1}=\boldsymbol\theta^{k}+\Delta t\,\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}^{k+1/2},\qquad
  \dot{\boldsymbol\theta}^{k+1}=\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}^{k+1/2}-\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\theta^{k+1}.
\end{equation}
Each step requires one tridiagonal product, i.e.\ $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations, with no precomputation beyond Algorithm~\ref{alg:tridiagonal}. The scheme is second order, symplectic and time-reversible; for linear systems it is stable for $\Delta t\,\omega_n<2$, and its energy error then remains bounded, without secular drift~\cite{hairer2006geometric}. The largest frequency $\omega_n$ is obtained in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations by bisection on the Jacobi matrix $\mathbf{J}$.

Monitoring the energy also does not require the dense $\M$. By Eq.~(\ref{eq:M-factor}),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy-On}
  \tfrac12\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}\trans\M\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}
  =\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\Bigl(\sum_{k\le i} l_k\dot\theta_k\Bigr)^{2},
\end{equation}
which is the kinetic energy written with the linearized horizontal velocity $\sum_{k\le i}l_k\dot\theta_k$ of mass $i$, and is evaluated in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations by a cumulative sum; the potential energy $\tfrac12\boldsymbol\theta\trans\K\boldsymbol\theta$ is diagonal.

Two limitations should be stated. First, Verlet is not exact: its phase and energy errors are $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$, whereas $\Phi(\Delta t)$ is exact up to round-off. Second, the stability bound ties $\Delta t$ to $\omega_n$, which grows as a uniform chain is refined, so the total cost of simulating a fixed time interval grows faster than linearly in $n$. The two methods are therefore complementary: $\Phi$ for small and moderate $n$ when maximal accuracy is required, Verlet when $n$ is too large for dense matrices.

\section{Exact Nonlinear Dynamics in \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{O}(n)$}{O(n)}}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

The tridiagonal structure is not restricted to small oscillations. Evaluating the exact accelerations from $\M(\boldsymbol\theta)\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta}+\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}$ by forming and factorizing the dense $\M(\boldsymbol\theta)$ costs $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ operations per evaluation. Recursive multibody algorithms reach $\mathcal{O}(n)$ for general serial chains~\cite{featherstone2008rigid,jain2011robot}; for a chain of point masses, a Newtonian formulation with the rod tensions as unknowns gives an explicit alternative that requires a single tridiagonal solve and connects directly to Theorem~\ref{thm:inverse}.

\subsection{Tension Formulation}

Let $\mathbf{e}_k=(\sin\theta_k,-\cos\theta_k)$ be the unit vector along link $k$, from mass $k-1$ to mass $k$ (mass $0$ is the pivot), $\mathbf{n}_k=(\cos\theta_k,\sin\theta_k)$ its normal, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ the upward unit vector, and $T_k$ the tension of rod $k$, with $T_{n+1}:=0$. Newton's law for mass $i$ and the kinematics of link $k$ read
\begin{equation}\label{eq:newton}
  m_i\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i=-T_i\mathbf{e}_i+T_{i+1}\mathbf{e}_{i+1}-m_i g\,\hat{\mathbf{y}},\qquad
  \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_k-\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{k-1}=l_k\ddot\theta_k\,\mathbf{n}_k-l_k\dot\theta_k^{2}\,\mathbf{e}_k,
\end{equation}
with $\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_0=\mathbf{0}$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact nonlinear dynamics in $\mathcal{O}(n)$]\label{thm:tension}
For any state $(\boldsymbol\theta,\dot{\boldsymbol\theta})$, the tensions solve $\mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol\theta)\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{r}$, where $\mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol\theta)$ is symmetric positive definite and tridiagonal,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tension}
  B_{kk}=\frac{1}{m_k}+\frac{1}{m_{k-1}},\qquad
  B_{k,k+1}=B_{k+1,k}=-\frac{\cos(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k)}{m_k},\qquad
  r_k=l_k\dot\theta_k^{2}+g\cos\theta_1\,\delta_{k1},
\end{equation}
with $1/m_0:=0$ and $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{0})=(\mathbf{E}\trans\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E})^{-1}$. The angular accelerations are then given explicitly by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nl-acc}
  l_k\ddot\theta_k=\frac{T_{k+1}\sin(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k)}{m_k}-\frac{T_{k-1}\sin(\theta_k-\theta_{k-1})}{m_{k-1}}-g\sin\theta_1\,\delta_{k1}.
\end{equation}
Both steps cost $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Dividing Eq.~(\ref{eq:newton}) for masses $k$ and $k-1$ by their masses and subtracting (for $k=1$, using $\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_0=\mathbf{0}$) gives
$l_k\ddot\theta_k\mathbf{n}_k-l_k\dot\theta_k^2\mathbf{e}_k=(-T_k\mathbf{e}_k+T_{k+1}\mathbf{e}_{k+1})/m_k-(-T_{k-1}\mathbf{e}_{k-1}+T_k\mathbf{e}_k)/m_{k-1}-g\hat{\mathbf{y}}\,\delta_{k1}$.
Taking the scalar product with $\mathbf{e}_k$ and using $\mathbf{e}_j\cdot\mathbf{e}_k=\cos(\theta_j-\theta_k)$, $\mathbf{n}_k\cdot\mathbf{e}_k=0$ and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\mathbf{e}_1=-\cos\theta_1$ yields Eq.~(\ref{eq:tension}); the product with $\mathbf{n}_k$, using $\mathbf{e}_j\cdot\mathbf{n}_k=\sin(\theta_j-\theta_k)$ and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_1=\sin\theta_1$, yields Eq.~(\ref{eq:nl-acc}). Moreover, $\mathbf{B}=\hat{\mathbf{G}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{G}}\trans$, where $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}=\operatorname{diag}(m_i\mathbf{I}_2)$ and row $k$ of $\hat{\mathbf{G}}\in\R^{n\times2n}$ contains $\mathbf{e}_k\trans$ in block $k$ and $-\mathbf{e}_k\trans$ in block $k-1$. Since $\hat{\mathbf{G}}$ is block lower bidiagonal with non-zero diagonal blocks, it has full row rank and $\mathbf{B}$ is positive definite. At $\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf{0}$ all cosines equal one and $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{-\mathsf{T}}$, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:inverse}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Consistency with the linear theory]\label{cor:linear-limit}
At rest, $T_k=gS_k$, the weight suspended below rod $k$. Linearizing Eq.~(\ref{eq:nl-acc}) about rest gives $\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta}=-\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\theta$, with $\mathbf{Q}$ the tridiagonal matrix of Corollary~\ref{cor:Q}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
At rest $\mathbf{r}=g\,\mathbf{e}^{(1)}$, so $\mathbf{T}=g\,\mathbf{E}\trans\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{e}^{(1)}$, whose $k$-th entry is $gS_{\max(k,1)}=gS_k$. Since $\dot\theta_k^2$ and $T_k-gS_k$ are of second order, to first order $l_k\ddot\theta_k=gS_{k+1}(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k)/m_k-gS_{k-1}(\theta_k-\theta_{k-1})/m_{k-1}-g\theta_1\delta_{k1}$. With $S_{k+1}=S_k-m_k$ and $S_{k-1}=S_k+m_{k-1}$, the coefficient of $\theta_k$ becomes $-gS_k(1/m_k+1/m_{k-1})/l_k=-Q_{kk}$, and those of $\theta_{k\pm1}$ coincide with $-Q_{k,k\pm1}$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:Q}).
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:tension} and Corollary~\ref{cor:linear-limit} thus close the circle: the inverse mass matrix of the linear theory is the zero-angle value of the tension matrix of the nonlinear theory, and the physical interpretation of Corollary~\ref{cor:Q} (nearest-neighbour coupling through rod tensions) holds exactly at all amplitudes.

\subsection{Structure-Preserving Nonlinear Integration}

Any explicit integrator applied to $\dot{\Rstate}=(\dot{\boldsymbol\theta},\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta})$ inherits the $\mathcal{O}(n)$ cost of Theorem~\ref{thm:tension} per stage. For long simulations a structure-preserving alternative is RATTLE~\cite{andersen1983rattle,leimkuhler1994symplectic}, applied to the Cartesian positions $\mathbf{q}_i$ with constraints $\phi_k(\mathbf{q})=\tfrac12(|\mathbf{q}_k-\mathbf{q}_{k-1}|^2-l_k^2)=0$ and Lagrange multipliers proportional to the tensions:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{q}^{k+1}&=\mathbf{q}^{k}+\Delta t\,\mathbf{v}^{k}+\tfrac{\Delta t^2}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q}^k)\trans\boldsymbol\lambda\bigr),\qquad \boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf{q}^{k+1})=\mathbf{0},\label{eq:rattle-q}\\
  \mathbf{v}^{k+1}&=\frac{\mathbf{q}^{k+1}-\mathbf{q}^{k}}{\Delta t}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q}^{k+1})\trans\boldsymbol\mu\bigr),\qquad \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q}^{k+1})\,\mathbf{v}^{k+1}=\mathbf{0},\label{eq:rattle-v}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{G}=\partial\boldsymbol\phi/\partial\mathbf{q}$ and $\mathbf{F}$ is gravity. RATTLE is second order, time-reversible and symplectic on the constraint manifold, and it enforces the rod lengths to solver tolerance at every step. Because constraint $k$ involves only masses $k-1$ and $k$, both the Newton matrix $\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q}^{k+1})\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q}^{k})\trans$ for $\boldsymbol\lambda$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:rattle-q}) and the symmetric matrix $\mathbf{G}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{G}\trans$ for $\boldsymbol\mu$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:rattle-v}) are tridiagonal, with the same pattern as $\mathbf{B}(\boldsymbol\theta)$. Each step therefore costs $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations per Newton iteration; about two iterations per step were needed in our tests.

\subsection{Numerical Results}
\label{sec:nonlinear-validation}

Figure~\ref{fig:nonlinear} summarizes the nonlinear results. (a) The tension formulation agrees with the dense formulation to a relative difference below $4\times10^{-12}$ for $n\le3000$; one evaluation takes $1.7\times10^{-4}$\,s at $n=3000$ against $0.68$\,s for the dense route (about $4000$ times faster), and $6.8\times10^{-3}$\,s at $n=10^5$.

(b) For a strongly chaotic test, the non-uniform triple pendulum of Section~\ref{sec:validation} is released from the horizontal position ($\theta_i(0)=\pi/2$, $\dot\theta_i(0)=0$) and integrated for $200$\,s; the energy error is normalized by $m_{\mathrm{tot}}gL_{\mathrm{tot}}$. RATTLE with $\Delta t=10^{-3}$\,s keeps the error bounded below $5.6\times10^{-5}$ with no growth ($3.1\times10^{-5}$ in the first tenth of the run, $2.7\times10^{-5}$ in the last). Classical RK4 with the same step is more accurate on this horizon but drifts, from $6\times10^{-10}$ to $1.1\times10^{-8}$; adaptive DOP853 drifts from $3.7\times10^{-6}$ to $9.6\times10^{-5}$ at tolerance $10^{-6}$ and to $1.4\times10^{-8}$ at $10^{-9}$. We therefore do not claim that RATTLE is more accurate than high-order methods over moderate horizons; its advantages are the absence of secular drift, exact preservation of the constraints and symplecticity, which matter for long-time and statistical studies. All four methods use the $\mathcal{O}(n)$ formulation.

(c) In the same chaotic regime, the RATTLE error at $t=2$\,s with respect to a DOP853 reference (tolerance $10^{-13}$) decreases with observed order $2.00$.

(d) As a cross-validation between the nonlinear and linear theories, a uniform $n=50$ chain is released from its fundamental mode shape with amplitude $\varepsilon$. The maximum deviation from the linear modal solution over $5$\,s, relative to $\varepsilon$, decreases as $\varepsilon^2$ (observed order $1.99$), as expected from the cubic leading nonlinearity.

\section{Conclusions}

This work presented a structural framework for the linear and nonlinear dynamics of the planar $n$-mass pendulum.  Its basis is the explicit matrix formulation, with a direct proof, of the cumulative mass-sum rule (Eq.~\ref{eq:prefix-sum}) and its systematic use as an organizing principle, including the factorization~(\ref{eq:M-factor}) of the mass matrix.  The factorization implies that the inverse mass matrix and the dynamic matrix $\M^{-1}\K$ are tridiagonal in closed form (Theorem~\ref{thm:inverse}, Corollary~\ref{cor:Q}), so the state-space operator is assembled in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations without solving any linear system, and that the problem is similar to a Jacobi matrix, which proves that the natural frequencies are simple and that the $k$-th mode has $k-1$ sign changes (Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum}). Numerically, the closed form is faster than the dense route by orders of magnitude, more accurate by about four orders of magnitude at $n=3000$, and reproduces the continuous hanging chain with $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})$ convergence. The same structure yields a symplectic St\"ormer--Verlet integrator with $\mathcal{O}(n)$ cost per step and an $\mathcal{O}(n)$ energy evaluation, complementing the exact but dense transition matrix when $n$ is large.  The standard generalized-eigenvalue solution and a scaled-and-squared matrix-exponential implementation agree with \texttt{scipy.linalg.expm} to double-precision round-off in the tested cases. Because the transition matrix is constant, it can be precomputed once and reused across many simulations.

For the nonlinear dynamics, the rod tensions satisfy a symmetric positive-definite tridiagonal system whose zero-angle value is the core $(\mathbf{E}\trans\mathbf{D}\mathbf{E})^{-1}$ of the inverse mass matrix (Theorem~\ref{thm:tension}), so the exact accelerations follow in $\mathcal{O}(n)$ operations and the linear theory is recovered as its small-amplitude limit (Corollary~\ref{cor:linear-limit}). The formulation is about 4000 times faster than the dense one at $n=3000$, and a RATTLE integrator built on the same tridiagonal structure is second order with bounded energy error in the chaotic regime.

Natural extensions include rigid links with distributed mass, joint damping and actuation for control design, implicit or exponential integrators~\cite{hochbruck2010exponential} for very fine chains, and experimental validation of the predicted spectra.

import numpy as np

def assemble_system(masses, lengths, g=9.81):
    """Assemble A, M, and K using precomputed suffix masses."""
    masses = np.asarray(masses, dtype=float)
    lengths = np.asarray(lengths, dtype=float)
    n = len(masses)
    S = np.cumsum(masses[::-1])[::-1]
    M = np.zeros((n, n))
    K = np.zeros((n, n))
    for j in range(n):
        for k in range(n):
            M[j, k] = S[max(j, k)] * lengths[j] * lengths[k]
        K[j, j] = S[j] * g * lengths[j]
    # Closed-form tridiagonal Q = M^{-1} K (Corollary 1), no linear solve.
    inv_m = 1.0 / masses
    inv_prev = np.concatenate(([0.0], inv_m[:-1]))
    Q = np.diag(g * S * (inv_m + inv_prev) / lengths)
    Q += np.diag(-g * S[:-1] * inv_m[:-1] / lengths[1:], -1)
    Q += np.diag(-g * S[1:] * inv_m[:-1] / lengths[:-1], 1)
    A = np.block([
        [np.zeros((n, n)), np.eye(n)],
        [-Q, np.zeros((n, n))]
    ])
    return A, M, K

def peano_baker_step(A, dt, N=20, eta=0.5):
    """Scaled-and-squared Taylor/Peano--Baker approximation."""
    B = A * dt
    norm_B = np.linalg.norm(B, ord=np.inf)
    s = 0 if norm_B <= eta else int(np.ceil(np.log2(norm_B / eta)))
    B = B / (2**s)
    dim = A.shape[0]
    Phi = np.eye(dim)
    term = np.eye(dim)
    for k in range(1, N + 1):
        term = (term @ B) / k
        Phi += term
    for _ in range(s):
        Phi = Phi @ Phi
    return Phi

def propagate_linear(Phi, R0, t):
    """Propagate linear system using precomputed transition matrix."""
    R = np.zeros((len(t), len(R0)))
    R[0] = R0
    for i in range(1, len(t)):
        R[i] = Phi @ R[i-1]
    return R
